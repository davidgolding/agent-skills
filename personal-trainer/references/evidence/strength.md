# Evidence Base: Strength & Resistance Training

Curated 2026-09-07. Load only when the request concerns resistance/strength training. If a request cites a specific recent study, a named novel method not covered here, or a claim that seems to contradict this file, perform a targeted lookup (Cached-First, Lookup-on-Gap pattern) and flag any contradiction found.

## Volume and frequency

- Weekly hard sets per muscle group is the primary volume driver for hypertrophy; a commonly cited working range is roughly 10-20 sets/week/muscle for most trained lifters, with beginners responding to less and advanced lifters sometimes tolerating more — treat this as a starting range to individualize against recovery, not a fixed target.
- Training a muscle group at least twice per week generally outperforms once-weekly training at equal weekly volume, because per-session volume stays within what can be executed with good quality.
- More volume is not unconditionally better — beyond an individual's recoverable ceiling, added volume degrades quality and recovery without adding adaptation (the volume-recovery tradeoff popularized in RP-style programming literature, e.g. Israetel/Helms).

## Intensity and proximity to failure

- Most hypertrophy-oriented work is effective in a broad rep range (roughly 5-30 reps) as long as sets are taken reasonably close to failure; rep range matters less than proximity to failure and total volume.
- Strength (as opposed to size) responds better to lower-rep, higher-load work due to specificity of the tested skill (the lift itself), even though hypertrophy can occur across a wide rep spectrum.
- RIR/RPE-based autoregulation (training a target number of reps short of failure) is a well-supported way to individualize intensity session to session — see `references/progression.md`.
- Training every set to absolute failure is not necessary for hypertrophy and increases fatigue cost and injury risk disproportionately; reserve true failure work for isolated, lower-risk exercises and sparingly.

## Progressive overload

- Long-term adaptation requires progressively increasing the demand placed on the muscle — via load, reps, sets, or density — over time; without progression, adaptation plateaus regardless of how well-designed a single session is.
- Double progression (fixed load until a rep target is hit, then load increases) is a simple, well-supported method for programmed progression — see `references/progression.md`.

## Periodization and deloads

- Both linear (progressively harder blocks toward a peak) and undulating (varied intensity/volume within a week) periodization models produce comparable outcomes for most recreational trainees; block structure matters less than consistent progressive overload and adequate recovery.
- Periodic deloads (reduced volume or intensity, typically every 4-8 weeks depending on how aggressively a block has been pushed) manage accumulated fatigue and reduce injury risk; they are a planned part of programming, not a sign of failure.

## Movement selection

- Compound, multi-joint lifts (squat, hinge, press, pull patterns) are an efficient base for most goals due to the volume of muscle mass trained per unit of time and fatigue.
- Exercise variation within a pattern (e.g., rotating bench variations) can manage joint stress and address individual weak points, but frequent, unstructured exercise-swapping undermines the ability to track progressive overload — hold movements reasonably constant within a block.

## Concurrent training interference

- Combining substantial endurance/HIIT work with strength work in the same block can blunt strength and hypertrophy gains relative to strength training alone, particularly when both are done at high frequency and volume; the effect is generally modest at moderate combined volumes and more pronounced at high volumes of both.
- Sequencing and spacing (strength before cardio in a session, or on separate days with adequate spacing) reduces the acute interference effect — see `references/evidence/hiit.md` for the HIIT-specific angle and `references/progression.md`'s frequency-ceiling rule.
