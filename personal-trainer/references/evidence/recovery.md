# Evidence Base: Recovery & Fatigue Management

Curated 2026-09-07. Load alongside another modality file when a request concerns fatigue, deloads, sleep's effect on training, or how hard a week has been — this file covers the cross-cutting recovery principles that inform programming rather than a modality of its own. If a request cites a specific recent study or claim that seems to contradict this file, perform a targeted lookup and flag any contradiction found.

## Sleep

- Sleep duration and quality materially affect strength, power output, reaction time, and next-day training readiness; consistent short sleep (well below typical adult recommendations of roughly 7-9 hours) measurably degrades training quality and recovery, independent of the training program's own design.
- A single poor night has a smaller acute effect than a sustained pattern of insufficient sleep — treat one bad night as noise, but a reported multi-day pattern as a genuine input to that week's autoregulation (favoring the lower end of a prescribed range, or a proactive deload).

## Fatigue accumulation and deloads

- Fatigue accumulates across a training block faster than fitness does early on, and the gap between them (functional overreaching) is a normal, planned part of progressive training — provided it's followed by planned recovery; unplanned or unmonitored accumulation without a subsequent deload is what tips into non-functional overreaching or overtraining.
- Deloads (planned reductions in volume or intensity, typically over one session to one week) are a standard tool for managing this accumulation and are built into the progression rules per modality (`references/progression.md`) rather than left to reactive judgment alone.
- Signals worth weighing together, not any single one in isolation: declining performance at a previously manageable load, elevated resting heart rate, disrupted sleep, reduced motivation, and lingering soreness beyond the usual 24-72 hour window.

## Recovery modalities

- Active recovery (light movement, easy aerobic work, mobility) generally aids subjective recovery and blood flow without materially impairing adaptation, and is a reasonable default for placing lower-effort sessions on days following the week's hardest work.
- Passive recovery aids (massage, foam rolling, contrast baths) have modest, mostly short-term effects on perceived soreness and readiness; useful as an adjunct but not a substitute for adequate sleep, nutrition-driven recovery, or appropriately managed training load.

## Illness and life stress

- Training through mild upper-respiratory illness (symptoms above the neck only) at reduced intensity is generally considered acceptable by common sporting-medicine guidance; systemic symptoms (fever, chest congestion, body aches, gastrointestinal illness) warrant stopping training until resolved, independent of any red flag in `references/safety.md`.
- Non-training life stress (poor sleep, high work stress, major life events) draws on the same recovery capacity as training stress; a week under unusual external stress is a reasonable trigger to favor the lower end of a prescribed range rather than pushing progression as usual.
